On 16 November I described the [theoretical studies](/en/journal/hybrid-keyboard-the-theoretical-studies/)
behind the new gaze keyboard of [Kite](/en/research/kite/), my open Augmentative and Alternative
Communication (AAC) tool. That entry had a single line about Zenodo. It deserves an entry of its own,
because behind that line there is a choice: I am not patenting the methods of the hybrid keyboard. I
have published them so that they belong to everyone.

In this entry I explain why, what a defensive publication is and what the two archives I chose are for:
Zenodo and Technical Disclosure Commons (TDCommons). Then I show, for the first time, Kite's keyboard at
work, in a video.

## The choice: publishing instead of patenting

A patent is an exclusive right over an invention. For a limited period, whoever owns it can prevent
others from exploiting it commercially ([WIPO, n.d.](#references)). It is a legitimate tool, and a
necessary one for many companies. But it goes in the opposite direction to this project.

SmartChair has a motto: breaking down the barriers to movement with open technology. Kite aims to be a
tool that anyone can use, study and adapt. A patent in my name would have made the keyboard's methods my
exclusive property: to use them in a product, people would have had to ask my permission.

Leaving them only in the journal, however, did not seem enough. The risk I fear is this: that someone
describes the same methods in a patent application, and that the patent office, not finding my journal,
grants it. It is a hypothesis, not something that has happened.

So I chose a third way: **defensive publication**. You describe an invention completely, publish it in a
public, dated place and make it easy to find. The purpose is precisely to prevent it from being patented
later ([Technical Disclosure Commons, n.d.-b](#references)).

It works thanks to a basic rule of patents: only a **new** invention can be patented. Under the European
Patent Convention, an invention is new if it does not form part of the **state of the art**. Article
54(2) defines it, in short, as everything made available to the public, in writing, orally or in any
other way, before the filing (or priority) date of the application ([EPO, n.d.-a](#references)). A
complete, public description becomes part of the state of the art. From that day on, anyone filing an
application on those same methods cannot present them as new.

An example. If tomorrow someone applied for a patent on selecting keys by counting time from when the
eye lands after a saccade, my description, published earlier, would be a document to cite against it:
if it describes what the application claims, that method is not new.

| | With a patent | With a defensive publication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who can exploit the methods, commercially too | the owner and whoever has their permission | anyone, except for existing third-party patents |
| Who can obtain a patent on those methods | whoever files an application first, if they are new | nobody: they are no longer new |
| What is left for me | an exclusive right | recognition, with my name and a date |

In one sentence: **the methods remain available to everyone, and nobody can present them as new in a
patent application any more.**

This applies to me too. The novelty rule applies regardless of who made the disclosure: in Europe, after
a publication, not even the author can patent what has been described
([EPO, n.d.-a](#references)), apart from very limited exceptions that do not apply here
([EPO, n.d.-b](#references)). I have chosen not to patent these methods anywhere. The description also
deliberately includes **variants and generalisations**, to reduce the room for patenting the closest
alternatives as new.

## What I have published

It is a technical description in English, with a courtesy Italian translation; if the two versions
differ, the English one prevails. It gathers the methods of the hybrid keyboard and their variants in a
single document, and it is written with the aim that an expert can reproduce them
([Blescia, 2026](#references)). I have already described the methods and their theoretical basis in the
[entry on the theoretical studies](/en/journal/hybrid-keyboard-the-theoretical-studies/): I do not
repeat them here.

The main methods have been public since **14 November 2026**, in the journal entries uploaded that day
to the public repository of this site. The full description, with the variants, has been public on
Zenodo since **16 November 2026**. The two archives make this publication solid, dated and easy to
find.

## Zenodo: preserving and citing

**Zenodo** is an open archive for research outputs in every discipline: papers, data, software. It was
created in 2013 by CERN, the European particle physics laboratory, and OpenAIRE, an open science project
commissioned by the European Commission ([CERN and OpenAIRE, 2013](#references)).

Three of its features matter for a defensive publication:

1. **It preserves.** Files are kept in CERN's data centres, in Geneva, with a replica in Budapest, and
   remain available for the lifetime of the archive ([Zenodo, n.d.-a](#references)).
2. **It does not rewrite.** The original content is never modified ([Zenodo, n.d.-a](#references)). If
   the document changes, a new version is published, and the first one stays as it was.
3. **It assigns a DOI.** A DOI (*Digital Object Identifier*) is a persistent identifier. It works like an
   address that never expires: even if the document moves, the DOI always leads to it
   ([DOI Foundation, n.d.](#references)). It is the same system used to cite scientific papers: many of
   the references at the end of the entries in this journal have a DOI.

On first publication Zenodo registers two DOIs: one for the specific version and one that represents all
versions and always leads to the latest ([Zenodo, n.d.-b](#references)).

Zenodo is the right place to **read and cite** the work: anyone who wants to build on these methods in a
paper or a project has a stable reference.

## TDCommons: reaching where patents are searched

A research archive preserves a document well, but I am not sure that someone examining a patent
application would look for it there. That is why the same description will also appear on
**Technical Disclosure Commons** (TDCommons).

TDCommons is an archive of technical descriptions, many of which are defensive publications. It is
operated by the company bepress at the request of Google
([Technical Disclosure Commons, n.d.-b](#references)). Its stated goal is to make these publications
accessible to the public and to patent offices around the world
([Technical Disclosure Commons, n.d.-a](#references)).

According to its frequently asked questions ([Technical Disclosure Commons, n.d.-b](#references)):

- publishing and searching are **free**;
- documents can be indexed by any search tool, including **Google Patents**, Google's search engine for
  patents, and this makes them easier to find for examiners too;
- a document is published **within 72 hours** of submission;
- the publication date is the upload date, and it is **printed on the front page of the PDF**.

Only the English description will appear on TDCommons. The document is not online yet: I will add the
link here as soon as it is published.

| | Zenodo | TDCommons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who runs it | CERN, with OpenAIRE | bepress, at the request of Google |
| What it offers | long-term preservation, content that does not change, persistent DOI | date printed on the PDF, indexable by Google Patents too |
| Who it serves most | people who want to read and cite the work | people who check whether an invention is really new before granting a patent |
| Where | [doi:10.5281/zenodo.24282465](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24282465) | coming soon |

The video is in Italian. It opens on **Kite's menu** (0:00), with the three components of the hybrid
selector all active: (1) prediction and dynamic touch areas; (2) modulation by pupillary load; (3)
Italian autocorrect and suggestions.

From about 0:15 comes the **pupil-luminance calibration**. The pupil responds both to light and to mental
effort: to use it as an index of effort, the effect of light must first be estimated and subtracted. So,
from 1:15, I look at a cross while the background goes from bright to dark. Around 1:50 a graph compares
the measured diameter (white dots) with the one predicted from light alone (orange line).

Until about 5:30 I explain the ideas of the keyboard out loud, and from 5:33 they can be seen at work,
with the **diagnostic view** on. The yellow dashed lines are the real boundaries of the keys: they widen
towards likely letters and shrink around unlikely ones, a bit like on smartphone keyboards. The
percentages on the keys are the probability of the next letter. At 5:45, after «vorrei un bic», C is at
53% and I at 32%: «bicchiere» (glass) or «bici» (bike).

When the gaze rests on a key, a **blue arc** closes around the letter. The time is no longer the old
keyboard's fixed second: it starts when the eye lands after a saccade and ranges from 120 to 700 ms
depending on the probability of the letter (the details are in the
[theoretical studies](/en/journal/hybrid-keyboard-the-theoretical-studies/#1-saccades-counting-time-from-when-the-eye-lands)).
The pupil acts only here: its index, cleaned of the effect of light, can lengthen the times when load
rises, and this is a hypothesis still to be tested. In the video, around 3:15, I simplify it.

Above the keyboard, the **suggestion bar** proposes three words: at 5:45 I choose «bicchiere» with a
single glance, and shortly afterwards «acqua» (water). Whatever the **autocorrect** does not fix on its
own also ends up there. The autocorrect, which I talk about around 2:45, steps in at the space: it
compares the selected letters with Italian words, taking the typical errors of the gaze into account.

The **grey completion** comes from sentences already said in the past. At 6:21, after «il mio nom», «e è
antonio» appears in grey: I fixate it for one second and the sentence is accepted (my name is antonio).
At 6:32 the same happens with «tu come ti chiami» (what is your name).

The **parla** (speak) key sends the text to Piper, a speech synthesiser that runs on the computer: it is
heard around 5:55, and at 6:03 you can see the arc closing on speak after «ho freddo» (I am cold). While
the voice is speaking, the text turns light blue and the keyboard selects nothing, so I can look at the
listener. Then the text is cleared; if I fix my gaze on the **cancella** (delete) key right afterwards, it brings
the text back, just as it undoes a correction or a sentence just accepted. In the last seconds the menu returns.

## Next step: the code

Publishing the methods serves to prevent them from being locked away with a patent. Publishing the code
makes it possible to actually use them.

That is why I will soon publish the **Kite repository**, with the code of the hybrid keyboard. The code
will have its own open source licence, which I will state when I publish the repository. Anyone will be
able to use it, study it, adapt it and **contribute freely to the growth of the project**: with a
report, a fix, a test or an idea. When the repository is online I will announce it here in the journal
and link it to the Zenodo record, so that the description and the code can find each other.
